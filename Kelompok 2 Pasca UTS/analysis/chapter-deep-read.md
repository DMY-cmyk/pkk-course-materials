## Learning Objectives (verbatim list, PDF p. 2)
After reading this chapter, you should be able to:
1. Understand the basic construction of the statement of changes in financial position.
2. Understand the motivation for the FASB's move from the statement of changes in financial position to the cash flow statement.
3. Understand the requirements of the cash flow statement and the nonarticulation problem that arises when using the indirect method.
4. Comprehend the classification problems that exist in the FASB's trichotomy of operating, investing, and financing activities.
5. Appreciate the analytical usefulness of the cash flow statement for different users.
6. Understand the importance and usefulness of free cash flow.

## S1: Chapter introduction / opening (PDF p. 2)
- Opening anecdote: the musical *The Music Man* (Meredith Willson) — salesmen advocating cash terms of sale ("Cash for the merchandise, cash for the button hooks..."); the point: bills, investments, debts, dividends are ultimately paid with cash, not earnings; a firm that does not generate sufficient cash faces extinction. "Cash is cash is cash." (p. 2)
- Historical heritage (p. 2): In 1971, the Accounting Principles Board (APB) mandated the **Statement of Changes in Financial Position (SCFP)** for financial reporting (a "funds flow" statement reporting changes in assets, liabilities, and owners' equity account balances). In 1987 the **FASB** mandated a **statement of cash flows (SCF)** in **SFAS No. 95**; this superseded the SCFP. The transition reflected the FASB's interest in cash-basis reporting as a supplement to the accrual-based income statement and balance sheet.
- Chapter roadmap (p. 2): next discusses SCFP plus motivation/structure of SCF; highlights two major problems — (1) the **nonarticulation problem** arising from use of the indirect method in the operating section, and (2) **classification problems / inconsistencies** with the FASB's three-way classification. Emphasis: the SCF should be considered **in its entirety**. Then introduces **free cash flow (FCF)** — a non-GAAP measure that developed partially because of SCF shortcomings — and concludes with a review of theoretical/empirical research plus recommendations for change.
- Definition introduced: SCFP — "funds flow" statement reporting changes in asset, liability, and owners' equity account balances (p. 2).

## S2: The Statement of Changes in Financial Position (PDF pp. 2–4)
- Key claims:
  - To appreciate the SCF, one must understand the structure/motivation of its predecessor, the SCFP (p. 3).
  - The SCFP itself is successor to the prior **funds flow statement**, in which working capital accounts are defined as comprising the **fund balance**; its purpose was to show how fund balance accounts increased from income/other sources and decreased from losses/other uses; the funds flow concept represented the liquid and readily available resources of the firm (p. 3).
  - **APB Opinion No. 19** stated the reporting objectives of the SCFP are to (a) complete the disclosure of changes in financial position, (b) summarize financing and investing activity, and (c) report funds flow from operations (p. 3).
  - The SCFP is a **derivative** financial statement — it relies on definitions/measurements of elements from the other two statements, providing a different way of classifying/reporting transactions (p. 3).
  - Two balancing sections: **sources of resources** (= transaction credits; arise from increases in equities and decreases in assets; increases in equities reflect financing from external sources e.g. debt/stock issues and internal sources e.g. net income and proceeds from disposal of assets) and **uses of resources** (= transaction debits; arise from decreases in equities and increases in assets; decreases in equities reflect reduction in firm's financing — debt retirement, treasury stock purchases, dividend payments, net losses; asset increases are uses of resources) (pp. 3–4).
  - In both sources and uses sections, transactions are classified into those affecting the fund balance and those affecting other accounts; net income's effect on fund balance reported separately (p. 4).
  - The funds flow statement (SCFP's predecessor) included only transactions under points 1a and 1b of Exhibit 13.1 (increases to fund balance from net income / other sources); transactions not affecting fund accounts were excluded → narrow liquidity focus. Adding point-2 transactions makes a comprehensive summary = the **all-inclusive** or **all-resources** SCFP. Point-2 transactions = investing/financing activities not affecting fund accounts (e.g., conversion of convertible debt to common stock, stock issued for nonmonetary assets, dividends paid in property rather than cash, nonmonetary exchanges of assets). APB Opinion No. 19 opted for the all-inclusive approach; a funds flow statement is still contained within the SCFP (p. 4).
  - APB Opinion No. 19 permitted any one of **four definitions of funds**: (1) cash, (2) cash plus near cash (short-term marketable securities and other temporary investments), (3) quick assets, (4) working capital — an example of flexibility. In all cases, all nonfund-account transactions are to be included in the SCFP even if no direct effect on fund accounts (p. 4).
  - When funds = working capital, nonfund transactions are restricted to nonmonetary transactions (nonmonetary exchanges, conversion of convertible debt to common stock). Defining funds as cash adds complications (many transactions don't affect cash → more nonfund transactions to report separately). A working-capital definition minimized cost of producing an SCFP; **most firms elected to define funds as net working capital** (p. 4).
  - Gradual recognition of SCFP importance: In **1963, APB Opinion No. 3** *recommended* inclusion of an SCFP in the annual report. The **SEC** made it mandatory for statutory filings beginning in **1971**. In response, the APB issued **APB Opinion No. 19 in 1971**, making the statement mandatory for financial reporting (p. 4).
- Definitions introduced:
  - Funds flow statement — working capital accounts comprise the fund balance (p. 3).
  - Sources of resources — transaction credits (p. 3).
  - Uses of resources — transaction debits (p. 4).
  - All-inclusive / all-resources SCFP (p. 4).
- Exhibits: Exhibit 13.1 (Standard Format of the SCFP) appears in this section (pp. 3–4).
- Numbered equation: (13.1) transaction credits = transaction debits (p. 3).
- Named citations: APB Opinion No. 19 (1971); APB Opinion No. 3 (1963); SEC (1971).

## S3: The Motivation for a Cash Flow Statement (PDF pp. 4–5)
- Key claims:
  - Question framed: what prompted the FASB to rediscover cash-basis accounting as a supplement and why did cash flow supplant the more general funds-flow/changes-in-financial-position concept (p. 4).
  - During FASB deliberations leading to the SCF, a consensus emerged that **funds should be defined as cash rather than net working capital** (p. 5).
  - Net working capital came to be viewed as a **poor measure of liquidity for three reasons**: (1) deferred charges and credits are included in net working capital but have no cash flow consequences; (2) conversion of current assets into cash can take a year or longer for firms with a long operating cycle; (3) items such as inventory are carried on a cost basis and thus do not explicitly measure the cash flow potential of the inventory (p. 5).
  - Cash flow reporting is appealing because of its straightforwardness and literal interpretation: "cash is cash is cash" (p. 5).
- No new exhibits/equations in this section.

## S4: Objectives of Financial Reporting (PDF p. 5)
- Key claims:
  - **SFAC No. 1** lists three general objectives of financial reporting. The first is broad: "Financial reporting should provide information that is useful to present and potential investors and creditors and other users in making rational investment, credit, and similar decisions." Two additional objectives are specific ways of meeting the first: (1) reporting information about the firm's net resources and changes in those resources, and (2) reporting information useful in assessing future cash flows. In the 1980s these two goals motivated the FASB's adoption of the SCF (p. 5).
- Citation: SFAC No. 1 (p. 5).

## S5: Objectives of the Statement of Cash Flows (PDF pp. 5–6)
- Key claims:
  - In **SFAC No. 5**, the FASB claims the SCF provides useful information about an entity's activities in generating cash through operations (to repay debt, distribute dividends, reinvest to maintain/expand operating capacity); about financing activities (debt and equity); and about investing/spending of cash. Uses of current cash receipts/payments info include assessing the entity's **liquidity, financial flexibility, profitability, and risk** (p. 5).
  - An earlier FASB discussion memorandum lists **six reasons cash flow data are a useful supplemental disclosure** (p. 5):
    1. Provide feedback on actual cash flows.
    2. Help identify the relationship between accounting income and cash flows.
    3. Provide information about the quality of income.
    4. Improve comparability of information in financial reports.
    5. Aid in assessing flexibility and liquidity.
    6. Assist in predicting future cash flows.
  - All six points deal with the **limitations of accrual accounting**; cash flow data supplement accrual data (p. 5).
  - Point 1: cash flow data necessary for assessing past cash flows. Point 2: necessary for understanding cash flow generated from *operations*. Point 3 — **quality of income**: a term analysts use for the relationship between cash flow and accounting income; the higher the correlation between accounting income and cash flows, the higher the quality of income; reflects awareness that accounting income comprises many noncash accruals/deferrals and may not indicate liquidity well (pp. 5–6).
  - Point 4 — **uniformity problem**: flexibility in choice of accounting policies harms comparability; cash flow from operations is subject to fewer arbitrary policy choices → more uniform, higher comparability; SCF advocated as a way of dealing with arbitrariness of income measurement; appealing clarity vs. abstractness of accounting income (p. 6).
  - Point 5 — financial flexibility and liquidity: **Financial flexibility** = the ability of the firm to adapt to new situations and opportunities. **Liquidity** = the capability for quick conversion of assets to cash without loss. Internally generated cash flow indicates both. Cash flow = internal resources for debt servicing/repayment, new investment, and distributions to stockholders — the original reason for requiring a funds flow statement (p. 6).
  - Balance sheet liquidity is limited: the current–noncurrent classification is a poor guide to liquidity (some current items are deferred charges/credits with no future cash flow impact; inventory may not be readily converted to cash; few current assets convert quickly; balance sheet measurement attribute is usually not net realizable value, so cash generated by assets cannot be determined). A balance sheet is "nothing more than a crude ranking of liquidity." SCF gives insight into cash-generating potential of operations (p. 6).
  - **Exit-price accounting** (illustrated in Appendix 1-A) is intended to measure flexibility (cash realizable from nonforced liquidation of assets); but even exit-price is only a crude indicator — speed of conversion ultimately determines liquidity/flexibility; firms raise capital incrementally rather than by selling all assets; usually use new capital or cash from held-for-sale assets (e.g., inventories) (p. 6).
  - Point 6 — predicting future cash flows: past cash flow data useful for predicting future cash flows, but it remains **unsettled** whether cash flow, funds flow, or accounting income is the best predictor of future cash flows; unclear what the prediction period should be; the expanded-disclosure philosophy maintains firms should disclose all potentially useful information (p. 6).
- Definitions introduced: quality of income (pp. 5–6); financial flexibility (p. 6); liquidity (p. 6).
- Citations: SFAC No. 5; FASB discussion memorandum (p. 5).

### S6a: Requirements / Structure of the SCF (PDF pp. 7–9)
- Key claims:
  - The SCFP used a very general "sources and uses" of funds framework — a binary framework focused mechanically on the narrow accounting debit–credit relation (Exhibit 13.1). In contrast, the SCF classifies cash receipts/payments into more meaningful categories: **operating, financing, and investing activities** (p. 7).
  - **Cash** = literal cash on hand or on-demand deposit, plus **cash equivalents**. **Cash equivalents** = short-term, highly liquid investments convertible into known amounts of cash. Like APB Opinion No. 19, the SCF requires all **noncash** investing and financing transactions to be reported as a supplement (in a schedule or narrative) — the all-inclusive/all-resources concept; presumably assures all transaction debits/credits are accounted for. Suggested format illustrated in Exhibit 13.2 (p. 7).
  - Cash flows segregated into operating, investing, financing activities → more classificational consistency / greater comparability than the SCFP. **Three of the seven FASB members dissented**: they maintained interest and dividends *received* arise from investing activities (per paragraph 22), and **interest paid** is an element of financing activities rather than an operating cost (per paragraph 23 of Statement 95); this is in line with current thinking in the finance discipline (p. 7).
  - **SFAS 95** allows operating cash flows by either the **direct method** or the **indirect method**. Direct method = reports literal cash flows related to income statement classifications (revenues, cost of sales, etc.); reports more information; FASB favors it. Indirect (reconciliation) method = starts with accrual income and adjusts for associated noncash items. FASB acknowledged the direct method can be **more costly** (not all companies organize records to produce the data). Not mentioned by FASB: in practice there is usually a **plug number** somewhere in the SCF to make numbers balance when using the indirect method (p. 7).
  - If the direct method is used, a separate schedule reconciles net operating cash flow with net income (Exhibit 13.3) — so the indirect/reconciliation method must be used either alone or as a supplement to the direct method. Several FASB members believe allowing the indirect method **impedes user understanding and diminishes quality of financial reporting**. Exhibit 13.3 also shows the supplemental schedule of noncash investing/financing activities required whether direct or indirect is primary (p. 7).
  - **The great majority of American firms use the indirect method.** Preparers seem influenced by the cost issue (strong preference for indirect). Both methods yield the same net cash flow from operations number; what differs is the information in arriving at it: (a) cash flow numbers for sales/COGS/etc. vs. (b) a reconciliation adjusting accrual income to its cash flow analog. The issue is one of costs vs. benefits (p. 9).
  - Limited empirical research indicates the **direct method is preferred** to the indirect, particularly by outside users. **McEnroe** study: 282 respondents (financial analysts, investment advisers, accounting professors, and "accountants") — **56% favored the direct method vs. 44% favoring the indirect** method. Direct method = operating section easy to understand but difficult to prepare; indirect method = the opposite (easy to prepare, harder to understand) (p. 9).
- Exhibits in this sub-section: Exhibit 13.2 (Illustration of the SCF per SFAS No. 95, Direct Method — "Company M") spanning pp. 7–8; Exhibit 13.3 (Indirect/Reconciliation Method) pp. 8–9.
- Selected Exhibit 13.2 (Company M, year ended Dec 31, 2000) numbers (pp. 8):
  - Operating: Cash received from customers $13,850; Cash paid to suppliers and employees (12,000); Dividend received from affiliate 20; Interest received 55; Interest paid (net of amount capitalized) (220); Income taxes paid (325); Insurance proceeds received 15; Cash paid to settle lawsuit for patent infringement (30); **Net cash provided by operating activities $1,365**.
  - Investing: Proceeds from sale of facility $6,001; Payment received on note for sale of plant 150; Capital expenditures (1,000); Payment for purchase of Company S, net of cash acquired (925); **Net cash used in investing activities $(1,175)**.
  - Financing: Net borrowings under line-of-credit agreement $300; Principal payments under capital lease obligation (125); Proceeds from issuance of long-term debt 400; Proceeds from issuance of common stock 500; Dividends paid (200); **Net cash provided by financing activities $875**.
  - **Net increase in cash and cash equivalents $1,065**; Cash at beginning of year 600; **Cash at end of year $1,665**.
- Selected Exhibit 13.3 (Indirect reconciliation, Company M) numbers (pp. 8–9):
  - Adjustments to reconcile net income to net cash: Depreciation and amortization $445; Provision for losses on accounts receivable 200; Gain on sale of facility (80); Undistributed earnings of affiliate (25); Payment received on installment note receivable for sale of inventory 100; Increase in accounts receivable (215); Decrease in inventory 205; Increase in prepaid expenses (25); Decrease in accounts payable and accrued expenses (250); Increase in interest and income taxes payable 50; Increase in deferred taxes 150; Increase in other liabilities 50; **Total adjustments $605**; **Net cash provided by operating activities $1,365**.

### S6b: The Nonarticulation Problem (PDF pp. 9–11)
- Key claims:
  - An important problem surfaced relative to the **indirect method**. An extensive study by **Bahnson, Miller, and Budge** found that where the indirect method is used to determine cash flow from operations, **nonarticulation** occurs when the cash flows arising from changes in the working capital accounts of consolidated enterprises are **not equal** to the working capital adjustments listed in the operations section of the SCF (p. 9).
  - Nonarticulation creates confusion in understanding the underlying numbers because it makes the SCF appear inconsistent with the underlying balance sheet. These discrepancies occurred in **75% of the situations** covered in the researchers' sample (p. 9).
  - Discrepancies are **not trivial**. **Worked example (Exhibit 13.4) — 3M Company, $000,000, fiscal year ended Dec 31, 2005:**
    - **Accounts receivable:** 2005 = $2,838; 2004 = $2,792; Change in balance sheet account = $(46); Working capital adjustment in the SCF = $(184); **Percentage difference = 300.0%**.
    - **Inventories:** 2005 = $2,162; 2004 = $1,897; Change in balance sheet account = $(265); Working capital adjustment in the SCF = $(294); **Percentage difference = 10.9%**.
    - **Accounts payable:** 2005 = $1,256; 2004 = $1,168; Change in balance sheet account = $88; Working capital adjustment in the SCF = $113; **Percentage difference = 28.4%**.
    - (Source noted: 3M Company 10-K annual report for fiscal year December 31, 2005, pp. 38, 40.) Note especially the AR percentage difference; the authors observed instances where even the **signs are different** (p. 10).
  - **Causes of nonarticulation:**
    1. **Acquisitions of subsidiaries during the year** — when a firm acquires a subsidiary, beginning-of-year working capital balances of acquired firms are not included in the beginning consolidated balance sheets; to articulate, these missing balances must be included in the consolidated firm's beginning-of-year balances (p. 10).
    2. Transactions involving working capital accounts that **do not affect cash** (affect nonconsolidated firms too): write-ups/write-downs of working capital items (typically inventories) when firms are acquired by purchase; depreciation allocations within manufactured inventories; any reclassification of working capital accounts between current and noncurrent categories (p. 10).
    3. One accounts payable account used for purchase of both **working capital assets** (inventories, prepaids) and **non-working-capital assets** (equipment, land, patents). E.g., purchase of inventory on account = operating activity → positive (cash inflow) working capital adjustment in operating section; purchase of equipment on account = investing activity → deferred payment reflected in investing section; so change in balance sheet accounts payable ≠ accounts payable adjustment in operating section (p. 10).
  - Related development: As part of the financial statement presentation project, in **2008 the FASB and IASB** issued a discussion paper proposing new presentation formats for the three financial statements; gives management significantly more discretion regarding classification of cash flows; improvements questionable, but combining operating and investing activities into a single "business" classification has merit (reduces managers' classification shifting between investing and operating activities) (pp. 10–11).
- Exhibit: Exhibit 13.4 (Comparison of Balance Sheet Changes and Working Capital Adjustments for 3M Company, $000,000) (p. 10).
- Citations: Bahnson, Miller, and Budge (p. 9); FASB/IASB 2008 discussion paper (pp. 10–11).

## S7: Classification Problems of SFAS No. 95 (PDF pp. 11–13)
This section contains sub-headings: International Accounting Standards (IAS); Premium and Discount on Bonds and Notes; Increased Flexibility of Presentation.
- Key claims:
  - Beyond the three FASB dissents, significant questions about SCF structure. Relative to the operating/financing/investing trichotomy, **Nurnberg** states the breakdown is at variance with the finance literature and supposed to provide info useful for investment/credit decisions. Nurnberg notes SCF classifies interest and dividend **receipts** as operating inflows and interest **payments** as operating outflows; per finance literature these are **investing** activities (receipts) and **financing** activities (payments) (p. 11).
  - In SFAS No. 95 the FASB followed the **income statement format with a proprietary orientation** — interest revenue/expense and dividend revenue as operating items. This contrasts with the **entity theory** approach (interest/dividend revenues = investing; interest expense = financing). The FASB may have faced a difficult choice between accounting-income (proprietary) and finance-orientation (entity); more practical considerations may have influenced it. **Banking institutions favored** classifying interest receipts and interest payments as operating items (which they may well be for banks) — to avoid reporting negative cash flows from operations. Consistency with the income statement plus the banking-industry problem may have influenced the FASB's split of interest expense (operations) from receipt/repayment of principal (financing) (p. 11).
- Definitions/concepts: proprietary theory vs. entity theory treatment of interest/dividends (p. 11).

### S7a: International Accounting Standards (IAS) (PDF p. 11)
- **IAS 7** of the IASB takes a flexible view toward interest/dividends received or paid — may be classified as operating, investing, or financing **provided they are classified consistently from period to period**. This conflicts with the proprietary orientation of SFAS No. 95 and the entity theory approach suggested in this chapter and Chapter 12. For banks, interest/dividends can be viewed as operating cash flows; if IAS 7 is changed to allow this classification by banks, the IASB would attain uniformity within industries (p. 11).

### S7b: Premium and Discount on Bonds and Notes (PDF pp. 11–13)
- The split between interest/dividend revenues (operating) and purchasing stock (investing) and borrowing/repaying principal (financing) leads to further problems. For bonds payable / long-term notes payable: how to handle discount or premium (interest adjustments that are part of principal borrowed — a positive amount for premium, a shortfall for discount).
- **Vent, Cowling, and Sevalstad** found **four ways** to deal with this in published annual reports (p. 11).
- **Worked numeric example (Exhibit 13.5):** a firm issues **four-year 8% coupon bonds** with total face/par value of **$10,000**, sold for **$11,000 on December 31, 2000** (i.e., a $1,000 premium). Assume straight-line amortization of the premium. **Annual interest expense = $550 ($800 coupon minus $250 of premium amortization)** (p. 11). The four methods (p. 12):
  - **Method 1** — easiest to follow; probably used with the direct method. Sum of operating flows does **not** equal accrual accounting interest expense because the $1,000 premium is part of the financing flow in 2000. (Method 1: 2000 financing inflow $11,000; operating flow $(800) each year 2001–2004; financing $(10,000) in 2004.)
  - **Method 2** — breaks the premium out of investment flow into the operating flow category; assigns the premium to operations in the **year of payment (2004)**. (2000 financing $11,000; operating $(800) 2001–2004; 2004 financing $(11,000); 2004 operating +$1,000.)
  - **Method 3** — like Method 2 but makes assignment in **2000, the year of issue**. (2000 operating $1,000 and financing $10,000; operating $(800) 2001–2004; financing $(10,000) in 2004.)
  - **Method 4** — allocates the premium **over the life of the bonds** (four years). (2000 financing $11,000; operating $(550) each year 2001–2004; financing $(250) each year 2001–2004; financing $(10,000) in 2004.)
  - **Authors' preference: Method 1**, despite lack of agreement between accrual and cash flow amounts. **Method 4 makes the least sense** (it allocates the premium over four years, breaking 2001–2004 annual cash flow into two $800 segments — i.e., misrepresents the actual cash flow). Method 1 almost assuredly used with direct method; Methods 1–3 all possible under the indirect method. Another example of the allocation problem (p. 12).
- Further classification issues (p. 12):
  - **Munter** raises similar issues: if interest payments are capitalized per SFAS No. 34, they are excluded from operating activities and included in investing activities as part of the acquisition costs of the fixed asset. Raises whether events should be classified by the **basic nature** of the receipt/expenditure (interest payment) or the **ultimate purpose** (asset acquisition).
  - Similar division in **lease transactions** (capital vs. operating leases): for operating leases, the entire cash expenditure is classified as a cash outflow from operating activities; for capital leases, the interest portion is operating and the principal reduction is financing. Whether basic nature or ultimate purpose should govern remains unanswered.
- Exhibit: Exhibit 13.5 (Premium Allocation Between Operating and Financing Cash Flows; Source: Based on Vent, Cowling, and Sevalstad 1995) (pp. 12–13).
- Citations: Nurnberg (p. 11); Vent, Cowling, and Sevalstad (1995) (pp. 11–12); Munter (p. 12).

### S7c: Increased Flexibility of Presentation (PDF p. 13)
- In SFAS No. 95, hedging activities are considered **investing activities** — strict classification by the nature of the hedge transaction (rigid uniformity concept) (p. 13).
- **SFAS No. 104** amends SFAS No. 95 and allows association with the balance-sheet account specific to the hedge transaction. If identifiable with a particular balance-sheet item such as inventories (e.g., hedging against inventory price increases), SFAS No. 104 allows the hedge (forward contracts, futures contracts, options/swaps) to be classified either with that item as an element of operations or as an investing activity. **Nurnberg and Largay** believe the increased flexibility generally leads to **less comparability** but may be justified as an increase in **"fineness"** in some situations. See FASB Financial Standards 230.10.45 for current SCF requirements (p. 13).
- Citation: Nurnberg and Largay (p. 13).

## S8: Analytical Usefulness of the Cash Flow Statement (PDF pp. 13–17)
Contains sub-headings: Issues Relating to Rules for Classifying Cash Flows; The SCF Is More Than Cash Flow From Operating Activities; Cash Flow Needs of Different Users.
- Key claims:
  - Despite classification/nonarticulation problems, the SCF is clearly very useful. Nicely demonstrated by research by **Ingram and Lee**, who use the income statement and SCF together. They posited that over time, **growing firms have *higher* income and *lower* cash flows from operations** — because growing firms have increasing inventories and accounts receivable as they expand; to some extent offset by increases in payables, but the net effect of growth in working capital is that each year the change in income exceeds the change in operating cash flows (in actual and relative terms). As a firm expands, there are net investment outflows (fixed assets acquired) and cash inflows from financing/new debt and equity, while dividends remain low or nonexistent (pp. 13–14).
  - For a **contracting** firm, relationships largely run in reverse: sales/income decline but operating cash flows usually increase (AR/inventory lowered); cash outflows for investment decline; in financing, cash outflows increase (stock repurchases/treasury stock, debt retirements, cash dividends increase) (p. 14).
  - **Ingram and Lee's statistical analysis** involved **almost 1,000 firms over the period 1974 to 1992**, largely supporting their deductive analysis; also found expanding firms likely have **more financial leverage** than contracting enterprises (p. 14).
- Citation: Ingram and Lee (pp. 13–14).

### S8a: Issues Relating to Rules for Classifying Cash Flows (PDF pp. 14–15)
- The SCF is less able to be manipulated than income but not exempt. Cash flow from operations is considered by many the most important part of the SCF; users focus on it. Consequently, activity is devoted to increasing cash flows from operations — by shifting cash outflows from operating to investing, or shifting cash inflows from investing to operating. Overall cash unchanged, but without scrutiny users view the realignment favorably (p. 14).
- **Maremont example — Tyco International** (home security alarm systems): customers pay ~$30/month; contracts often extend beyond a year. Tyco sells these services itself but also buys service contracts from other dealers. Cash expended to develop home-security-alarm sales vs. money spent to acquire service contracts from other firms should be treated similarly: (a) cash outflows from operations for portions applicable to home security alarm sales recognized in income in current year, and (b) cash outflows for investments for the portion attributable to revenues recognized in future periods — well-merited rigid uniformity. **However**, Tyco treats them differently: dealer contracts purchased are treated as "acquisitions" and recognized on the SCF as cash outflows from operations on a considerably slower basis than cash outflows from internally generated home security alarm systems. Classificational inconsistencies of this type are not unusual (p. 14).
- **Mulford example (Wall Street Journal) — automotive industry:** **Ford Motor Co., General Motors Corp., and Harley-Davidson Inc.** lent money to dealers as notes receivable so dealers could purchase inventories. Companies recognized revenue from sales but classified the loans as **investing activities** → increased reported cash flow from operating activities by a nontrivial amount. In the **General Motors** case, reported cash flow from operating activities was **$7.6 billion**; if the notes receivable had been treated as an **operating** activity instead of investing, cash flow from operating activities would have been **$3.5 billion** (pp. 14–15).
- Contrast — **Navistar International Corp.:** while the captive subsidiary shows notes receivable as investing, Navistar's **consolidated** results reclassify the notes receivable as part of cash flow from **operating** activities. **Mark Oberle** (Navistar director of investor relations): "we define ourselves as a manufacturing company with a finance subsidiary... [The] sale and the associated change in receivables... [is] part of our core business" (p. 15).
- Upshot: even when classification rules are not violated, vagueness/flexibility in applying the rules allows companies in the same industry to give vastly different perspectives; comparability may be weak at best. A partial solution follows (p. 15).
- Citations: Maremont (Tyco) (p. 14); Mulford / Wall Street Journal (p. 14); Mark Oberle, Navistar (p. 15).

### S8b: The SCF Is More Than Cash Flow From Operating Activities (PDF pp. 15–16)
- The undue attention to one section of the SCF (CFO) is problematic; the SCF consists of three parts and all are important.
- **WorldCom Inc. example:** On **July 21, 2002**, WorldCom filed voluntary petitions for reorganization under Chapter 11 (U.S. Bankruptcy Code, Southern District of New York). Prior to filing, WorldCom engaged in accounting irregularities, **capitalizing expenses that should have been classified as operating activities** — this misclassification boosted reported cash flow from operating activities and lent credibility to net income. While many investors were fooled, the SCF in its entirety gave enough hints to cause skepticism (p. 15).
- **Exhibit 13.6** presents selected WorldCom SCF items until ~6 months before bankruptcy. Net income was positive for the three years before filing. If cash flow from investing reflects necessary investments, the difference between CFO and CFI shows how much cash is/will be available. WorldCom was hemorrhaging cash in three of the four years before bankruptcy; cumulative effects: by Dec 31, 2001, the company had dug itself a **$12 billion hole** (p. 15).

### S8c: Cash Flow Needs of Different Users (PDF pp. 16–17)
- **Warren Buffett's 1988 letter to Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA) shareholders** summarizes what needs to be reported: data that helps financially-literate readers answer **three key questions**: (1) Approximately how much is this company worth? (2) What is the likelihood that it can meet its future obligations? (3) How good a job are its managers doing, given the hand they have been dealt? (p. 16).
- Financial statements need info for: valuing the company, making credit decisions, assessing managerial performance. Assessing managerial performance requires accurate measures of **historical** cash flows; credit/valuation decisions require unbiased predictions of **future** cash flows (per SFAC No. 1) (p. 16).
- Investment decisions depend on **predicting future cash flows**. Investing is a capital allocation decision; accepted criterion = same as capital budgeting: accept an investment if the present value of expected net cash flows is positive — i.e., accept if NPV is positive. A stock purchase is acceptable if the per-share present value of future expected cash flows (**intrinsic value**) is greater than the current market price (p. 16).
- **Warren Buffett (Berkshire Hathaway "An Owner's Manual")** on intrinsic value: "Intrinsic value is an all-important concept that offers the only logical approach to evaluating the relative attractiveness of investments and businesses. Intrinsic value can be defined simply: It is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life." Calculation is not simple — it is an estimate, not a precise figure, and must change if interest rates move or forecasts are revised (pp. 16–17).
- Definitions: intrinsic value = the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life (p. 17).
- Citations: Warren Buffett 1988 BRKA shareholder letter (p. 16); Buffett "An Owner's Manual" (pp. 16–17); SFAC No. 1 (p. 16).

## S9: Free Cash Flow (FCF) — definitions and ABC illustration (PDF pp. 17–21)
(This material flows directly from S8c; the discussion of FCF and the ABC Company illustration occupies pp. 17–21. No standalone "Free Cash Flow" header is shown but the content is a distinct topic block.)
- Key claims & definitions:
  - The cash flows used to determine intrinsic value are, for the firm, called **free cash flows (FCFs)**. **Mulford and Comiskey** note: "The term 'free' refers to an absence of a superior claim. It is cash flow that is available for use with no strings attached. Spending it will not affect the firm's ability to generate more." (p. 17).
  - Mulford and Comiskey document increasing attention to FCF in the financial press, likely a "reaction to the numerous and well-publicized accounting problems and examples of egregious acts of earnings management witnessed in recent years"; they view the popularity positively: "We welcome the growing attention placed on free cash flow. We think that it provides evidence that investors and creditors are emphasizing what really matters to analysis and valuation." (p. 17).
  - Several definitions of FCF exist; following the **entity theory** of the firm, the authors focus on **cash flow to the firm**. FCFs **cannot be obtained directly from the SCF**. Defined as **(13.2): FCF = NOPLAT − investment in operating invested capital** (p. 17).
  - **NOPLAT** = net operating profit less adjusted taxes; in a simple case, NOPLAT = after-tax operating income. Investment in operating invested capital is typically broken into investment in net operating working capital and investment in noncurrent assets (p. 17).
  - FCFs **do not include interest expense** (a financing expense). Consistent with an ongoing firm, **operating cash is regarded as part of net operating working capital** (like AR and inventories) (p. 17).
  - Illustration via **ABC Company**: Exhibit 13.7 = income statement and balance sheet; Exhibit 13.8 = SCF constructed from those two statements; Exhibit 13.9 = statement of free cash flows; Exhibit 13.10 = how FCFs can be computed from the SCF (add back after-tax interest expense to CFO to eliminate financing effects, and subtract the increase in operating cash as part of invested capital); Exhibit 13.11 = comparison of four performance/valuation measures (p. 17).
  - **Exhibit 13.11 / four-measure guidance** (guidance text p. 18; Exhibit 13.11 table p. 21): choice of measure depends on time, resources, and specific use. Some users can consider **net income** only. For better understanding of operations and assessing quality of net income → focus on **cash flow from operating activities (CFO)**. To avoid problems classifying operating expenses as investing and to get a sense of *net* cash-generating ability → subtract cash from investing activities (CFI), i.e., **CFO − CFI**. Because CFO is "contaminated" with interest expense (financing), to focus on operations and recognize operating cash as part of invested capital → compute **FCFs**. Investors can estimate intrinsic value by discounting forecasted FCFs using the firm's **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** (p. 18).

## S10: Cash and Funds Flow Research (PDF p. 21)
- Key claims:
  - Two long-standing advocates of cash flow reporting, **Lawson and Lee**, argue that cash flow reports are necessary to report on the firm's performance — liquidity (cash flow) is an integral part of performance. **Lee** more strongly: "Cash flow and not profit is the end result of entity activity. Profit is an abstraction; cash is a physical resource." (p. 21).
  - Some debate whether cash flow reports are **superior** to accrual statements or just an **important supplement**; both Lawson and Lee make a strong case. The cash flow valuation model from the financial-economics literature presents a similar viewpoint: cash flows of the firm are the ultimate determinant of firm value, not accrual accounting income. However, a growing body of capital-market research shows accounting **accruals** provide information over and above literal cash flows vis-à-vis security prices (p. 21).
  - One interpretation: cash flows and accruals are **more useful together** than either alone; the SCF is **complementary** to accrual statements; decomposing accrual data into cash flow and accrual components provides *new* information (p. 21).
  - Surveys of investors/analysts consistently show cash (funds) flow data are used for investment analysis but conventional profitability analysis based on accrual data **dominates** over the liquidity focus. However, a survey commissioned by the **Financial Accounting Foundation (FAF)** found funds flow data **increasing in importance** while accrual data **decreasing in importance**. Suggests cash flow plays an important and perhaps increasing role in assessing firm performance/prospects (p. 21).
- Citations: Lawson and Lee (p. 21); Financial Accounting Foundation survey (p. 21).

## S11: Improving the SCF (recommendations, incl. Broome) (PDF pp. 21–22)
- Key claims:
  - The SCF is an important tool for investors, creditors, and managers; valuable in current form but room for improvement (p. 21).
  - **Broome** notes the SCF plays a crucial role in securities analysis. Reflecting on accounting debacles of **Adelphia, Dynegy, Qwest, Tyco, WorldCom**, and others, he argues the SCF can and should be improved. SFAS No. 95's flexibility in allowing either direct or indirect method creates confusion. Broome: "The complicated adjustments required by the indirect method are hard for the reader to understand and... provide corporate managers more leeway for manipulating the statement of cash flows.... In many cases, these adjustments cannot be reconciled to observed changes in balance sheet accounts." (pp. 21–22).
  - **Broome's three major recommendations** (p. 22):
    1. Given that both indirect and direct methods might have valuable information content, the FASB should **require the direct method *and* the associated reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow** for the operating section.
    2. The FASB should provide **more guidance on classification** of cash flows for the three sections.
    3. "The supplementary reconciliation should **begin with cash flow from operations and proceed to net income**, which is the reverse of the current practice in statements of cash flows."
  - **Authors' own recommendations** (p. 22): they **agree with Broome that the direct method should be required**. However, if the present system of choice is kept, firms should be required to provide a **schedule showing the noncash flow transactions that affect working capital accounts** (to let users understand the differences between balance sheet changes and the SCF). Furthermore, for **mid-year acquisitions**, firms should provide a **schedule that reconciles the working capital adjustments in the operating section with the respective balance sheet changes**. In general, a firm should **explain the source of any nonarticulation** that occurs in the operating section of the SCF.
- Citation: Broome (pp. 21–22).

## S12: Summary (PDF pp. 22–23)
- Key claims (summary/conclusion section):
  - The SCFP was mandated by APB Opinion No. 19 in 1971 — to disclose changes in financial position, summarize financing/investing activity, and report funds flow from operations (p. 22).
  - The SCF is a **special case within the more general SCFP**, with funds defined as cash. It is a **derivative statement** (based on transactions already summarized in income statement and balance sheet). However, new information is reported through decomposition of data into cash flow and accrual components, and through reclassifying data into operating flows, net financing flows, and net investment flows (p. 22).
  - In SFAS No. 95, the FASB chose the traditional income statement, **proprietary** approach rather than the entity model → classification issues: interest expense, interest revenue, dividend revenue all appear in the operating section, whereas related balance sheet items (bonds payable, stock investments, long-term notes receivable) are financing/investing elements. The entity approach might be more useful, but the FASB stayed with the traditional income statement model (p. 22).
  - Two issues associated with the SCF: **nonarticulation** and **misclassifications**. Nonarticulation = working capital adjustments in the operating section frequently differ from changes in the respective balance sheet accounts; can occur with the indirect method (p. 22).
  - Misclassification types (p. 23): one type mixes financing cash flows (interest expense) with operating cash flows; another type increases CFO by shifting certain cash flows to investing activities. International accounting standards allow flexibility in presentation as long as consistency is maintained (p. 23).
  - Research on usefulness to investors of cash/funds flow data is supportive that they are informative above and beyond accrual data; some argue SCFs are more useful in investment analysis than earnings reports. Yet problems remain: either direct or indirect method can be chosen; if direct is selected, an indirect reconciliation must also be presented — this led to an almost unanimous selection of the **indirect** method (p. 23).
  - **Free cash flows** are growing in usage; may be considered a variant of the SCF, consistent with classifying interest expense as a financing activity (p. 23).
  - Despite the problems, the change from SCFP to SCF brought greater consistency among firms; provides info useful for predictive purposes and enhances comparability; the authors expect the SCF to become increasingly important because it does not contain the "arbitrariness" of the income statement (p. 23).
